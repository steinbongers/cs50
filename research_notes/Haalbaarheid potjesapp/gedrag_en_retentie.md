# Gedrag en retentie: handmatig categoriseren, budgetapps, streaks en Nederlandse jongvolwassenen

Scope: behavioral evidence for one-tap manual categorization of every bank transaction into "pots", plus retention of finance apps, streaks/reminders, and Dutch 18-30 money behavior. Research done 2026-10-09. Note on method: most sources were seen via search summaries or abstracts; items marked "(fetched)" were read directly. Classic papers marked "(not re-checked this session)" are cited from established literature with DOI and were not re-opened.

## 1. Manual tracking, self-monitoring, pain of paying, mental accounting: effect on awareness and spending

### Takeaway
There is consistent but mostly observational evidence that looking at and recording your own spending lowers discretionary spending and fees. The best causal evidence (Levi & Benartzi, Journal of Finance 2026; Carlin, Olafsson & Pagel, Review of Finance 2023) is about *easier access to/monitoring of* transaction data, not about manual categorization specifically. No RCT was found that tests "tap every transaction into an envelope" against a control group.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhang (2023), Consumer Interests Annual vol. 69 (fetched, extended abstract, Univ. of Wisconsin PhD work):** Study 1 used user data from a Chinese app where users *manually* record income/expenses and set spending limits. Expense tracking was associated with a lower share of discretionary spending, more budget slack, and budget adjustments. Study 2 (US survey) found that financial self-awareness mediates the link between tracking and saving, and that self-awareness can induce the pain of paying. Comparing methods: "Automated tracking, with automatic collection of spending data through bank accounts or financial tools, is convenient but linked to lower attention and less financial self-awareness. In contrast, active tracking through manual expense recording requires more engagement and is associated with higher financial self-awareness." The extended abstract gives no effect sizes, and the design is correlational, so selection is likely: people who track manually may already be more conscientious. — [Zhang 2023, CIA](https://www.consumerinterests.org/assets/docs/CIA/CIA2023/ZhangYilingCIA2023.pdf). A related dissertation is in the UW repository (did not load, HTTP 503) — [UW repository](https://asset.library.wisc.edu/1711.dl/63XZYPDLKMY638S/R/file-908a3.pdf)
- **Levi & Benartzi, "Mind the App" (Journal of Finance, published online 30 Sep 2026; SSRN working paper 2020):** Uses account-aggregator transaction data. Identification comes from people who had used the same service on PC and then installed the mobile app, plus the staggered release across iPhone, iPad and Android. Discretionary spending *decreased* after installing the mobile app. The decrease was larger in evening hours and among people with lower self-control proxies, which the authors read as a monitoring/self-regulation channel. The 2020 version also reported more frequent logins (especially at retail peak hours) and stronger effects for lower-income and high spending-to-income users. — [Journal of Finance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.70087); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3557689)
- **Carlin, Olafsson & Pagel (Review of Finance 27(3), 2023; earlier NBER WP 23798, 2017):** Studied Iceland's Meniga aggregator app, whose mobile app launched 14 Nov 2014. Easier access led to more frequent checking of transactions and balances, and to a significant drop in high-interest unsecured debt (overdrafts) and bank fees. A secondary source puts the effect at about 14% less debt per extra monthly login over 2 years (check against the published version). Millennials and Gen X benefited, Baby Boomers did not. — [IDEAS/RePEc, Review of Finance](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/revfin/v27y2023i3p977-996..html); [NBER WP 23798](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23798/w23798.pdf); [Rice Business summary](https://business.rice.edu/wisdom/can-mobile-banking-app-really-save-you-money)
- **Ostrich effect (Olafsson & Pagel, AEA 2018):** People log into their financial accounts less often when balances are low or after bad news (selective attention). This is a risk for a daily review habit precisely when money is tight. — [AEA conference paper "The Ostrich in Us"](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2018/preliminary/paper/bQ8Q9AFE)
- **Pain of paying and payment friction:** In a field study of cash, credit and stored-value contactless payments, the payment process significantly affected subjective transactional awareness and payment pain. Payment method affected how precisely people knew their spending, but not their memory of it. — [ScienceDirect, Information & Management 2018/19](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378720618301496). "Paying in a blink of an eye: it hurts less, but you spend more" (J. Econ. Behav. & Org., 2024) found lower pain of paying with low-friction payments. — [ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268124001100). Overview — [Wikipedia: Pain of paying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pain_of_paying)
- **Classic theory (not re-checked this session):**
  - Prelec & Loewenstein (1998), "The Red and the Black", Marketing Science: the pain-of-paying / mental accounting model — [doi:10.1287/mksc.17.1.4](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.17.1.4)
  - Soman (2001), J. Consumer Research: payment modes that need "rehearsal" (writing, more salient) improve recall of past spending and reduce later spending — [doi:10.1086/319621](https://doi.org/10.1086/319621)
  - Prelec & Simester (2001), Marketing Letters: credit card versus cash willingness to pay — [doi:10.1023/A:1008196717017](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008196717017)
  - Heath & Soll (1996): mental budgeting. People assign expenses to budget categories, and tracking makes a category feel "used up", which curbs further spending in it. Incompletely tracked expenses lead to over- or under-consumption — [doi:10.1086/209465](https://doi.org/10.1086/209465)
  - Thaler (1985/1999): mental accounting and earmarked "envelopes" — [Thaler 1999, J. Behav. Decision Making](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0771(199909)12:3%3C183::AID-BDM318%3E3.0.CO;2-F)
- **Self-monitoring in health:** Zhang (2023) builds on self-monitoring evidence in weight loss (Burke et al. 2011 systematic review, J. Am. Diet. Assoc., doi:10.1016/j.jada.2010.10.008). That review links consistent self-monitoring to better outcomes. The transfer to finance is by analogy only. — cited in [Zhang 2023](https://www.consumerinterests.org/assets/docs/CIA/CIA2023/ZhangYilingCIA2023.pdf)

### Inferences
- A one-tap categorization step is a plausible form of "desirable friction" or rehearsal (Soman 2001). It forces at least one moment of attention per transaction, after the fact. That makes it closer to the monitoring channel in Levi & Benartzi and Carlin et al. than to point-of-sale pain of paying, because categorizing happens after payment, often hours later. It probably raises awareness but will not directly hurt at the moment of purchase.
- The strongest causal evidence supports "more frequent looking at your transactions → lower discretionary spending/fees", with larger effects for low-self-control and lower-income users. The app's target group (18-30) is in the group that benefited in Carlin et al.
- Heath & Soll's mental budgeting predicts that seeing a pot fill up curbs spending in that category. Displaying pot totals right after a tap is therefore theoretically important, more than the tap itself.

### Gaps
- No RCT or quasi-experiment found that isolates *manual categorization* against passive/automatic categorization, with spending as the outcome. Zhang 2023 is correlational and only an extended abstract.
- No effect sizes for YNAB-style envelope budgeting found in peer-reviewed literature. YNAB's own claims (vendor) were not searched or verified.
- The magnitude of spending reduction in Levi & Benartzi (2026 version) was not visible in the abstract.

## 2. Does automation reduce engagement/awareness compared with active categorization?

### Takeaway
There is weak-to-moderate evidence that passive/automatic tracking goes with lower attention and self-awareness (Zhang 2023, correlational), and that low-friction payments reduce spending salience. No direct experiment was found. Automation is not unambiguously worse: the causal studies show benefits from *passive* aggregator apps simply through easier viewing.

### Cited Findings
- Automated tracking (bank-linked) is "linked to lower attention and less financial self-awareness", while manual recording is "associated with higher financial self-awareness" (survey analysis). — [Zhang 2023](https://www.consumerinterests.org/assets/docs/CIA/CIA2023/ZhangYilingCIA2023.pdf)
- A small qualitative preprint (12 participants, India; not peer-reviewed) describes a "temporal gap between spending and awareness" with digital payments and cites earlier work that automated payments feel less painful and are less salient. Treat as weak evidence. — [Preprints.org "Spendception"](https://www.preprints.org/frontend/manuscript/afbca510121e1391f76c72f55ed4cec1/download_pub); published version [MDPI Behavioral Sciences 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/3/387)
- The counter-evidence is that passive aggregator apps (Meniga, US aggregator), which categorize automatically, still reduced overdrafts, fees and discretionary spending through more frequent checking. — [Carlin et al. 2023](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/revfin/v27y2023i3p977-996..html); [Levi & Benartzi 2026](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jofi.70087)
- Practical drawback of auto-categorization: a patent (US 8,924,393, Intuit-type) states that correcting a wrong automatic categorization "takes more user time than it would have taken to manually enter the correct categorization". This is a vendor/patent claim, not empirical research. — [USPTO patent 8924393](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8924393)

### Inferences
- The best-supported design position is a hybrid: keep the active one-tap as the awareness moment, but use suggestions or pre-selection to cut effort. Whether pre-selection erodes the awareness benefit is untested and could be an A/B test in the pilot.
- The key mechanism in the causal literature is *frequency of looking*. A daily sort session mainly works if it drives regular viewing. The tap is a means to that, not proven to be essential in itself.

### Gaps
- No HCI study found comparing completion and awareness between manual and auto-categorized flows in a finance app.
- Accuracy rates of bank-transaction auto-categorization (PSD2 data in NL) were not researched here.

## 3. Retention benchmarks for budgeting/finance apps and reasons for churn

### Takeaway
Finance apps typically keep about 26-30% of users on day 1, about 15-19% on day 7 and about 4-12% on day 30. Sources disagree, and nearly all figures come from vendor blogs that repeat AppsFlyer/Adjust numbers. Primary benchmark reports could not be accessed. Day 90 for finance is generally in the low single digits. No reliable day-90 figure was found for budgeting apps specifically. Churn drivers are effort/time cost, loss of motivation, poor fit and data/trust problems.

### Cited Findings
- **Finance category, 2025-2026 vendor compilations (secondary, citing AppsFlyer/Adjust-type data):**
  - About 26.8% on day 1, 18.5% on day 7 and 8.0% on day 30 — [UXCam 2026](https://uxcam.com/blog/mobile-app-retention-benchmarks/); [Plotline](https://www.plotline.so/blog/retention-rates-mobile-apps-by-industry)
  - Other blogs give finance day-30 figures of 4.2%, 4.6% or 3.1% (iOS) — [GetPanto 2026](https://www.getpanto.ai/blog/mobile-app-retention-statistics); [Apsteq 2026](https://apsteq.com/blog/app-retention-benchmarks/)
  - Higher fintech estimates: day-1 median 28% and day-30 median 7%, with top performers at 35-45% on day 1 and 10-15% on day 30 — [GetStream 2026](https://getstream.io/blog/app-retention-guide/). Others claim finance/banking day 30 of 12-18%, or fintech day 30 of 15-25% citing AppsFlyer 2023 — [Growth-onomics 2026](https://growth-onomics.com/mobile-app-retention-benchmarks-by-industry-2026/); [Blockchain-ads 2025](https://www.blockchain-ads.com/post/app-retention-benchmarks)
  - Cross-category day-30 median is about 4-7% — same sources
  - Weak/vendor evidence: the higher numbers probably mix in banking apps (high natural retention) with budgeting tools.
- **Health-app abandonment (best peer-reviewed analogue):** A scoping review (JMIR 2024, 18 studies) found 22 reasons for abandonment in six groups: technical/functional issues, privacy, poor UX, content/features, time and financial cost, and changing needs/goals. A median of 70% of users stopped within the first 100 days. — [PubMed 39693620](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39693620/); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11694054/)
- A survey of mHealth users found amotivation, loss of interest and trying other apps were the main abandonment drivers — [PMC8872344](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8872344/)
- A grounded-theory study of 47 app abandoners found seven reasons: functional value, information expectation, emotional value, platform service, trust, cost and social influence — [Emerald, Library Hi Tech](https://www.emerald.com/lht/article-abstract/43/2-3/622/1274393/An-exploratory-study-of-factors-influencing-user?redirectedFrom=PDF)
- **Budgeting-specific (weak):**
  - A Finnish bachelor thesis found students see budgeting as "too restrictive" — [Theseus thesis](https://www.theseus.fi/bitstream/10024/889760/2/Le_Nguyen_Ha.pdf)
  - Design blogs point to maintenance burden ("logging purchases, reconciling, reviewing categories... becomes tedious"); opinion only — [Onething Design](https://www.onething.design/post/budget-app-design); [Vocal](https://vocal.media/education/why-digital-budgets-fail-understanding-the-struggle-with-budgeting-apps)

### Inferences
- A realistic pilot target is about 25-30% day-1 and about 8% day-30 as "category average". Anything above about 15% day-30 would be good for a non-banking finance app. These anchors are soft.
- "Tracking fatigue" is the main structural risk of a categorize-every-transaction design. The backlog grows on days the user skips, and a large backlog likely triggers avoidance (compare the ostrich effect in section 1). A cap or "skip all/later" option and batching would address the documented effort cost.
- PSD2 consent expiry (re-authentication needed periodically) and sync gaps fall under "technical/functional" and "information expectation" churn reasons. This is an inference; not researched here.

### Gaps
- Primary AppsFlyer/Adjust/Sensor Tower finance-category reports could not be retrieved, so all figures above are second-hand.
- No published retention curves for YNAB, Mint, Monefy, Dyme, Grip or similar budgeting apps found.
- No reliable day-90 benchmark for finance apps found.

## 4. Streaks, gamification and daily reminder notifications

### Takeaway
Streaks raise engagement while they are intact, but a broken streak lowers motivation below baseline. Streak repair options soften this. Daily push reminders give a short-term boost that fades over about 3-4 weeks through habituation, and prompts/cues do not reliably build habits. Duolingo's own evidence is A/B-test based but vendor-reported.

### Cited Findings
- **Silverman & Barasch (2023), "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", J. Consumer Research (doi:10.1093/jcr/ucac029):**
  - Over multiple studies, including language learning and games on MTurk, intact streaks increased engagement, and a broken streak demotivated people who had adopted the streak goal
  - Simply being told a streak was intact (with identical actual behavior) increased motivation
  - A repair option attenuated the post-break drop
  - Silverman advises apps to avoid "you broke your streak" messages and to offer ways to keep streaks alive
  - Sources: [Psychology Today 2023](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); [Univ. of Delaware](https://lerner.udel.edu/seeing-opportunity/lerner-professor-researches-how-streaks-motivate-us/); [Phys.org 2024](https://phys.org/news/2024-03-streaks.html); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/361658509_On_or_Off_Track_How_Broken_Streaks_Affect_Consumer_Decisions)
- **IDB working paper "Streaking to Success":** Highlighting streaks to students affected effort and achievement (education field setting). — [IDB](https://publications.iadb.org/publications/english/document/Streaking-to-Success-The-Effects-of-Highlighting-Streaks-on-Student-Effort-and-Achievement.pdf)
- **Duolingo (vendor, primary blog):** A "Streak Wager" A/B test gave statistically significant increases in day-1, day-7 and day-14 retention, with day-7 up 14%. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)
- **Unverified Duolingo claims:** The widely repeated "7-day streak users are 2.4x more likely to return" could not be traced to a primary source and is correlational. Claims that streak freeze reduced churn by 21% are unsourced. — e.g. [Deconstructor of Fun](https://duolingo.deconstructoroffun.com/mechanics/streaks)
- **HeartSteps micro-randomized trial (Klasnja et al., Annals of Behavioral Medicine 2019; fetched):**
  - Design: 37 sedentary adults analysed, 6 weeks, up to 5 randomized decision points per day
  - Overall effect: any suggestion raised 30-minute step count by about 14% (35 steps, p=.06); walking suggestions by about 24% (p=.02)
  - Decay: the effect was about 66% on day 1, fell by about 2-3% per day and was undetectable by about day 28-29. Participants said the messages became repetitive after 2-4 weeks (habituation)
  - Source: [Annals of Behavioral Medicine](https://academic.oup.com/abm/article/53/6/573/5091257)
- **myBPmyLife MRT (JMIR 2025):** About one activity notification per day did not increase steps in the following 60 minutes (ratio 1.01, 95% CI 0.98-1.04). — [JMIR doi:10.2196/78218](https://doi.org/10.2196/78218); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12779098/)
- **HabitWalk MRT (Baretta et al., 2025):** Cites a meta-regression finding no significant effect of "prompts and cues" on physical-activity habit formation. — [Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being](https://iaap-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aphw.12605)
- **MRT methods paper:** Repeated notifications risk habituation and, if burdensome, disengagement. — [arXiv 2005.05880](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.05880)

### Inferences
- The planned daily streak with an evening reminder fits the evidence for short-term lift. Expect the reminder's marginal effect to fade within about 4 weeks unless content varies, for example by showing the number and amount of unsorted transactions rather than a fixed text.
- Because broken streaks demotivate, the streak should be forgiving:
  - count a "day" as "inbox empty or nothing new"
  - offer a freeze/repair
  - avoid shame messaging
- A streak tied to *sorting* rather than *spending* is safer: it does not reward or punish financial outcomes, only the habit.
- Daily cadence may be more than needed. The causal finance studies show benefits from more frequent checking, but none tested daily versus weekly.

### Gaps
- No finance-specific trial of streaks or daily reminders found.
- No data on optimal reminder time (for example 20:00) for finance apps.
- Independent evidence on Duolingo streak freeze effects not found.

## 5. Financial behavior of Dutch/European 18-30-year-olds

### Takeaway
Dutch young adults are under rising financial pressure: 54% of 18-30-year-olds found it hard to make ends meet in 2026, up from 40% in 2024 (Nibud). Teenagers already struggle to keep track of Tikkie payment requests and BNPL. Literacy is low for a large minority. No Dutch figures were found on what share of 18-30-year-olds budget or use budgeting apps.

### Cited Findings
- **Nibud "Geldzaken in de praktijk 2026" (via NOS, 23 Jun 2026; fetched):**
  - 18-30-year-olds who find it hard to make ends meet: from 40% (2024) to **54% (2026)**, the steepest deterioration of any group
  - All households: 38% find it hard to make ends meet (32% two years earlier); 25% could not pay bills this year (17% two years earlier)
  - Almost a third have ≤€2,500 in savings, and almost a quarter <€1,000
  - Overdrafts and payment arrangements are relatively common
  - Source: [NOS 2026](https://nos.nl/artikel/2619854-nibud-huishoudens-zeggen-vaker-financieel-in-de-knel-te-komen)
- **Nibud "Betaalgedrag scholieren 2025" (about 1,600 secondary school pupils; NOS 5 Mar 2025; fetched):**
  - 1 in 3 struggle to keep track of payment requests (Tikkie), and they send about 20 requests per month on average
  - 1 in 3 say a request sometimes goes unpaid, and half don't send a request for every amount
  - 13% use BNPL despite the 18+ rule; almost a quarter of users sometimes pay late
  - AFM counted about 600,000 BNPL purchases by minors in 2023
  - Many don't track their spending
  - Sources: [NOS 2025](https://nos.nl/artikel/2558314-nibud-jongeren-verliezen-door-nieuwe-betaalmethoden-financieel-overzicht); [Nibud report PDF](https://www.wijzeringeldzaken.nl/platform-wijzeringeldzaken/publicaties/rapport-betaalgedrag-scholieren-nibud-2025.pdf); [Nibud news](https://www.nibud.nl/nieuws/financieel-overzicht-lastig-voor-scholieren/)
- **Nibud Studentenonderzoek 2024 (mbo/hbo/wo, ages 16-30):** A quarter of mbo students have debts or payment arrears, with health-insurance arrears rising, and 79% of BOL students live at home. — [Tweede Kamer document](https://www.tweedekamer.nl/downloads/document?id=2024D32954); [open.overheid.nl](https://open.overheid.nl/documenten/dpc-79ec409243725c8ec174a110cf0ec494ece1aa7d/pdf)
- **Deloitte / Nibud / Tilburg / Leiden financial health monitor (via NJi):** The financial health of 18-24-year-olds worsened in 2024. 47% of 18-24-year-olds reported consumer debt in 2024 (56% in 2023), including informal loans and open accounts. — [NJi cijfers](https://www.nji.nl/databanken/cijfers/cijfers-over-schulden-betalingsachterstanden); [NJi nieuws](https://www.nji.nl/actueel/jongere-staat-er-financieel-slechter-voor)
- **Nibud via NJi:** Young adults (18-27) are more financially vulnerable than average, and almost 40% say they struggle to make ends meet monthly (earlier measurement). — [NJi preventieve aanpak](https://www.nji.nl/kennis/armoede-en-geldzorgen/preventieve-aanpak-van-geldzorgen-bij-jongeren)
- **Wijzer in geldzaken, financial literacy (Feb 2023):** 45% of 18-25-year-olds are not financially literate, and 30% say they are more impulsive than the average Dutch person. — [Wijzer in geldzaken 2023 PDF](https://www.wijzeringeldzaken.nl/platform-wijzeringeldzaken/publicaties/m221032-rapport-financiele-geletterdheid-bij-volwassenen-v3.pdf)
- **Wijzer in geldzaken youth (16-19) studies:**
  - March 2026 "Geldsprookjes": temptations and skills — [PDF](https://www.wijzeringeldzaken.nl/platform-wijzeringeldzaken/publicaties/onderzoek-geldsprookjes-te-mooi-om-waar-te-zijn-maart-2026.pdf)
  - March 2024 "Hoe blijf jij je geld de baas" — [PDF](https://www.wijzeringeldzaken.nl/platform-wijzeringeldzaken/publicaties/onderzoek-hoe-blijf-jij-je-geld-de-baas-11-3-2024.pdf)
  - Specific budgeting percentages were not extracted from these.

### Inferences
- Demand side: rising financial stress among 18-30-year-olds (54%) and teens' documented trouble keeping track of Tikkie/BNPL point to a real need for overview.
- Payment requests and refunds (Tikkie inflows) should be first-class in the sorting flow, because they distort the picture for this age group.
- High stress also raises ostrich-effect risk. Users who most need the app may avoid opening it when balances are bad, so messaging should be non-judgmental.

### Gaps
- No Dutch figure found for the share of 18-30-year-olds who budget, keep a budget overview, or use a budgeting app. Check the full Nibud "Geldzaken in de praktijk 2026" report (nibud.nl returned 403) and the Nibud Studentenonderzoek.
- No Tikkie usage statistics for 18-30 (for example ABN AMRO user numbers) were retrieved.
- AFM/DNB/CBS youth publications were not reached within the search budget. The AFM BNPL figure is via NOS only.
